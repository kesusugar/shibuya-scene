## STEP 1 — What the ground query actually was

The audit found one thing, and it was disqualifying.

`ctx.height(x,z)` — the only surface query the player had — **rounds its argument to a quarter
of a metre and memoises the result**:

```js
const a=Math.round(x*4)/4,b=Math.round(z*4)/4;   // before
```

That is the right call for two thousand pedestrians, who each need a height every frame and
none of whom is being looked at closely. Under a foot it is a 25 cm staircase: walk forward and
the surface under your sole jumps in steps a quarter of a metre wide, which no amount of IK can
smooth because the input itself is a stair. So `heightExact` was added beside it, sharing the
same body, and `height` became the rounded memo of it. The crowd is unaffected — same
arithmetic, same cache, same cost.

**There are no ground normals.** The world is polygons plus a height function; nothing stores a
surface orientation. The normal is therefore estimated from two finite differences at a 12 cm
span, which is the ankle's own width — a smaller span reads the query's own precision, a larger
one smooths the ramp away.

That estimate has one failure that matters. **A kerb and a ramp produce the same difference.**
A 13 cm rise over a 12 cm span is a 47° "slope" if you believe it, and a foot rolled onto a 47°
plane at the lip of a kerb reads as a broken ankle. So a difference larger than 5.5 cm over that
span is classified as an *edge* and the surface is reported flat. Shibuya's only real slope is
the kerb ramp at a crossing — `GROUND.rampDepth` 1.5 m for a 13 cm rise, 5.0° — which is
far below the threshold and survives.

Surfaces, from `src/ground/config.mjs`: road 0.02 m, kerb 0.15 m, ramp 1.5 m long. The step a
foot has to deal with is **13 cm**. The two thresholds are chosen so they agree with each other:

| | rise over the 12 cm span | verdict |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| kerb ramp (13 cm over 1.5 m) | 10.4 mm | slope — 5.0°, tilt the ankle |
| kerb (13 cm over nothing) | 130 mm | edge — report flat, leave the ankle alone |
| `normalStep` 55 mm, the line between them | 55 mm | 24.6° |
| `maxAnkleTilt` 0.42 rad, the most a sole may roll | | 24.1° |

The steepest surface the sampler will call a slope and the steepest roll the ankle will accept
are the same angle to within half a degree. That is not a coincidence worth leaving implicit:
if `normalStep` were raised without raising `maxAnkleTilt`, the sampler would start reporting
slopes the ankle then refuses to stand on, and the cap would silently become the real threshold.

## STEP 2–6 — The solver

Five pieces, in the order they run each frame.

**Stance** (`stanceWeight`). RUN 4 gave every clip one shared phase aligned on the left foot's
contact, so stance is arithmetic: the left foot is planted from phase 0 to `duty`, the right
one `offset` later. `duty` and `offset` are blended from the clips by the same weights the
legs are (`gait.stance()`), because a walk's 0.404 duty mixed with a run's 0.329 should give
something between. The edges are faded over 16 % of a cycle — an instant switch pops even when
the position it switches to is correct.

**Both feet are not sucked to the ground.** A swinging foot has weight 0 and is not touched.
Standing is the exception and it is deliberate: `stance()` reports `duty: 1` when nothing is
moving, which is true — a standing person has both feet down — and without it a standing
character waits for a step that is never coming and stands inside the kerb.

**Ground sample** (`sampleGround`), described above: height plus an edge-rejecting normal.

**Two-bone IK** (`createTwoBoneSolver`). The standard interior-angle solve — the same one
`three-player-controller`'s `twoBoneIK.ts` implements — in metres, in world space. The one
choice worth stating: **the bend plane comes from where the animation put the knee.** A solver
that picks its own bend direction is a solver that decides mid-stride that this knee bends
sideways. Taking the plane from the animated pose is what makes "respect the animation" a
mechanism rather than a slogan.

**Pelvis** (STEP 6). When one foot's ground is lower than the other's, the hips come down
rather than the leg stretching. Damped at 6.5 /s, bounded at 17 cm, and **added to what the
animation just wrote, never assigned** — every bone in these clips has an animated translation,
and assigning an absolute pelvis height replaces the walk cycle's own rise and fall with a
constant.

The hips take the **whole** drop rather than a share of it, and that is measured rather than
asserted — see the sweep below.

**Ankle** (STEP 5). After the solve, the sole's **animated world orientation is restored**, and
the ground normal is then rolled onto that, capped at 24° by slerping part of the way back to
upright rather than refusing the tilt outright. Because edges report flat, the roll itself does
nothing anywhere except on a kerb ramp; the restore matters everywhere the pelvis moves.

## Two bugs that agreed with themselves

Both were found by measuring the world matrix rather than the counters, and both had already
been written up in this file as limits of the character asset. They were not.

**The pelvis drop was applied along the wrong axis.** The code read
`pelvis.position.y -= drop`. This skeleton is authored **Z-up** — the pelvis bone's rest
position is `(0.005, 0.086, 0.877)`, and the 0.877 is the height — with a wrapper node rotating
the armature into the scene's Y-up. So subtracting from the bone's local `y` moved the hips
**thirteen centimetres backwards and not one millimetre down**, while `stats.pelvisDrop` went on
reporting a 130 mm drop, because it was reporting the number it had asked for. Shoving the
pelvis backwards also drags the feet, so the contact error moved, a little, in the right
direction, for entirely the wrong reason — which is how it survived a first round of
measurement. The fix converts a world-space down vector into the pelvis's parent frame and
divides by the parent scale, so it is correct for whatever axis convention the next character
arrives with. That matters: `CharacterAsset` exists so a photorealistic GLB can replace this
one, and a hard-coded axis is a trap set for that day.

**The solve was free to pitch the planted foot.** A two-bone solve turns the thigh and the
shin; the foot is rigidly parented to the shin, so it rotates by however much the knee had to
bend. Dropping the hips 130 mm to reach the low foot swung the *planted* foot's toe 58 mm down
into the pavement while its ankle sat exactly where the solver had aimed it. The solver aims the
ankle; the viewer watches the sole. Fixed by capturing the ankle's animated world orientation
with the anchor and restoring it after the solve, with the ground roll applied on top of that
rather than on top of whatever the solve left behind.

Together these two were the whole of the "residual": the earlier draft of this note reported
a lower foot stranded 86–111 mm in the air and blamed the Idle clip's locked knees. With both
fixed, a character astride a 13 cm kerb has **both** feet at the same contact error flat ground
gives them. There was no animation limit. There were two bugs, and a set of counters that
agreed with each of them.

Both now have tests that fail when the bug is put back — verified by putting each back.

## STEP 3–4 — Why there is no horizontal foot lock

The brief asks for a foot plant lock, and one was built: remember where a foot landed, pull it
back there while it is planted, abandon the pull past a limit. **It was removed after
measuring.** On flat pavement it *doubled* the contact error it was meant to remove, and it
visibly fought the walk cycle.

The reason is RUN 4. A foot lock exists to hide the slip you get when stride length and ground
speed disagree; RUN 4 made `period = blended stride / actual ground speed`, so they agree, and
there is very little slip left to hide. What the lock actually did was pin a foot while the
body walked past it, which over-extends the leg and then snaps.

So the vertical correction stayed and the horizontal one went. The "lock" that remains is a
**height** lock: a planted foot is held at its own ground, and the abandonment rule
(`maxReach` 34 cm, and a solve whose residual exceeds it is dropped) applies to that.

This is the clearest case in this run of the priority order holding: perfect foot contact lost
to natural animation, on measurement rather than on taste.

## STEP 9 — When the solver stands down

`figure.mjs` runs the solver last, on the finished pose, and refuses to run it at all when the
feet are not walking on anything:

| condition | why |
| --- | --- |
| `Fall`, `Death` | knocked down; the animation is deliberately not grounded |
| `Enter`, `Exit`, `Drive` | the figure is being moved by the vehicle, not by its feet |
| `state.alive === false` | as above |
| `state.riding` | as above |
| a jump over 1.2 m in world XZ in one frame | a teleport or a respawn, not a stride |
| `ctx.heightExact` absent | no surface to query; the baked eleven-bone figure also takes this path |
| `asset.legBones` absent | the skeleton does not name the joints; no IK is built at all |

Standing down clears the counters as well as the state. That is not cosmetic: a solver that has
stood down but still reports the legs it solved three frames ago makes every test downstream of
it lie, which is how it was caught.

## STEP 7 — The kerb test (measured)

`node qa/gta-upgrade/footik-contact.mjs`. No renderer, so these reproduce on any checkout.
Error is measured at the **ball** of the foot, not the ankle: ankle-to-sole distance changes
with ankle pitch, which would let a tilted foot read as a floating one. Negative is into the
ground. `off` is the same code with the solver switched off, not an older commit.

| surface | off L | off R | off mean | on L | on R | on mean | solved | pelvis |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| flat pavement | −7.6 | −7.0 | **7.3** | −7.6 | −7.0 | **7.3** | 0 | 0 mm |
| flat road | −7.6 | −7.0 | **7.3** | −7.6 | −7.0 | **7.3** | 0 | 0 mm |
| kerb, astride | 122.4 | −7.0 | **64.7** | −7.6 | −7.0 | **7.3** | 2 | 130 mm |
| kerb ramp, mid | 7.3 | −28.9 | **18.1** | −7.9 | −7.4 | **7.6** | 2 | 16 mm |

Read that as three separate results.

- **Flat ground is bit-identical.** Not "close" — the deadzone means nothing is solved at all,
  and the pose that comes out is the pose the animation produced. That is the whole reason
  `deadzone` exists and it is the first thing that regresses.
- **The 7.3 mm floor is the model, not the solver.** The soles sit about 7 mm below the surface
  in every case, on and off, because the character's sole is a mesh with a thickness and the
  ball bone is inside it. It is the noise floor of this measurement, not an error.
- **A kerb is fully corrected.** A standing character astride a 13 cm kerb goes from one foot
  **122 mm in the air** to both feet at the same 7 mm the flat ground gives. The residual
  attributable to the kerb is zero.

### The pelvis drops but never rises

A deliberate asymmetry. `lowest` is clamped to zero, so a foot whose ground is *higher* than
the body's gets no help from the hips and the leg folds up to reach it on its own.

Both cases occur in Shibuya, because which one you get depends on where the body's own centre
sits: a character astride a kerb with their centre over the pavement has a foot that must reach
down, and with their centre over the road has one that must reach up. The photographic bench
happens to produce the second (its body centre lands on the road side) and measures a **10.7 mm
mean** contact error there, from 72.3 mm — so the leg alone handles it, and adding a pelvis
rise would buy little while risking the opposite failure, a stretched lower leg. One-sided is
the conservative choice and the measurement supports it.

### How much of a kerb should the hips take?

Measured, not assumed. The solver's view of the surface is clamped, so it cannot ask for more
than the cap; error is still measured against the real kerb.

| cap | pelvis | low foot | high foot | mean | worst |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 mm | 0 | 122.4 | −7.0 | 64.7 | 122.4 |
| 20 | 20 | 102.4 | −7.0 | 54.7 | 102.4 |
| 40 | 40 | 82.4 | −7.0 | 44.7 | 82.4 |
| 60 | 60 | 62.4 | −7.0 | 34.7 | 62.4 |
| 80 | 80 | 42.4 | −7.0 | 24.7 | 42.4 |
| 100 | 100 | 22.4 | −7.0 | 14.7 | 22.4 |
| **130** | **130** | **−7.6** | **−7.0** | **7.3** | **7.6** |
| 170 | 130 | −7.6 | −7.0 | 7.3 | 7.6 |

Monotonic to the full kerb height, and the high foot never pays for it — it stays at −7.0 mm
the whole way down, because the restore keeps it planted. An earlier draft tried 0.55 of the
drop on the theory that a person straddling a kerb dips but not by the whole 13 cm; the
measurement says otherwise, and so does standing on a kerb. `maxPelvisDrop` at 170 mm is a
safety rail — a 13 cm kerb asks for 13 cm and the clamp never engages.

## STEP 7 — The kerb test (photographed)

`qa/gta-upgrade/footikbench.html`, which walks or stands the real controller and the real
figure over synthetic Shibuya surfaces and samples the stance foot every frame. The solver
counters are totals over the whole run, not the last frame — a scenario that walks off a ramp
onto flat road ends doing nothing, and reading the final frame reported a solver that had
never run.

| scene | off mean / p95 | on mean / p95 | leg-solves | ground queries |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| kerb, astride | 72.3 / 137.0 | **10.7 / 23.8** | 151 (1.00 per frame) | 906 |
| kerb ramp, walking down | 13.7 / 27.2 | **12.1 / 25.8** | 40 (0.23 per frame) | 690 |
| flat pavement, walking | 12.1 / 24.8 | 12.1 / 24.8 | 0 | 498 |
| flat road, running | 49.0 / 133.2 | 49.0 / 133.2 | 0 | 543 |

The two flat rows are the ones to read carefully. **The solver ran** — 498 and 543 ground
queries — **and solved nothing**, because on flat ground the surface under the foot and the
surface under the body are the same and the correction is zero. That is the design working,
not the design missing. It is also why the counters are in the header: without them, "the IK
is on and nothing changed" cannot be told from "the IK was never switched on".

The kerb's `worst` of 132.3 mm is the first few frames, before the correction has faded in at
`blendRate` 9. It is a startup transient, not a steady-state error.

## The largest remaining contact error is the Run clip, and RUN 5 does not touch it

Running on flat road measures 49.0 mm mean and 142.4 mm worst, identical with the solver on.
Following that number to its cause is the most useful thing in this run for deciding RUN 6.

| gait | speed | sole's lowest point | the clip's real contact band | the `duty` window |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| walk | 1.50 | −25.5 mm | phase 0.02 … 0.26 | 0.00 … 0.40 |
| run | 4.20 | **−42.9 mm** | phase 0.07 … 0.18 | 0.00 … 0.35 |

Two separate facts, both of them the animation's and neither of them the solver's.

- **The Run clip's sole passes 43 mm through the road at its lowest.** On flat ground the
  solver's correction is zero by construction, so the clip is shown exactly as authored, feet
  through the tarmac and all. A foot IK that aimed the ankle at an absolute height above the
  surface would hide this — and would throw away the heel-to-toe roll, which is the trade this
  run declined on measurement in STEP 3–4. It is a real visible defect and it is now located.
- **The `duty` window is wider than the clip's real contact**, by roughly 3× for the run. RUN 4
  measured stance as "near its own lowest **and** moving backwards relative to the hips", and
  in a bounding jog the foot satisfies that for much longer than it is actually down. On flat
  ground this is harmless — a zero correction is zero however long it lasts — but on a kerb it
  lets the solver correct a foot that is swinging. It is **not** retuned here: adjusting RUN 4's
  measured gait data on the strength of one character is exactly the overfitting the brief
  rules out, and the fix belongs with whatever replaces these clips.

So the honest summary of contact quality after RUN 5 is: **the ground is handled, the clips are
not.** Every error that comes from the surface — the 13 cm kerb, the ramp — is gone. Every
error that comes from the animation is exactly where it was.

## What is still not fixed

- **The 7 mm sole offset.** Every sole sits about 7 mm below its surface, solver or no solver,
  because the ball bone is inside a mesh that has thickness. Correcting it means measuring the
  mesh rather than the bone; it is below the threshold of visibility and it is not worth an
  extra per-frame term. Recorded so the next person does not read it as an IK error.
- **Ground query returns 0 off the network.** `heightExact` falls back to 0 where a point is
  neither road nor pavement polygon — inside a building footprint, or in a gap between
  corridors. That is pre-existing behaviour shared with the crowd, but the crowd never places
  a foot on it. A foot that samples such a point is asked to reach 15 cm down; `maxReach`
  catches it at 34 cm and the abandonment rule applies, so the failure is a foot that does not
  correct rather than a leg that folds — but it is a real edge and it is why the in-scene check
  below samples the surface around the player rather than trusting it.
- **Nothing is applied to NPCs.** Player only, as specified. STEP 10 estimates the cost of
  extending it; nothing is extended.

## The RUN 4 leftovers, re-evaluated

RUN 4 left six things unpolished and the instruction was to re-evaluate them after foot IK
rather than fix them during it. None is fixed here. What changed is that three of them are now
measured instead of described.

**Foot sliding.** `node qa/gta-upgrade/footik-contact.mjs`, third table — how far a sole
travels across the ground while it is planted:

| gait | speed | slide, IK off | slide, IK on | as % of a step |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0.5 m/s | 0.49 | 37.5 mm | **37.5 mm** | 12.6 % |
| 1.0 m/s | 0.99 | 38.2 mm | **38.2 mm** | 6.4 % |
| walk | 1.50 | 89.6 mm | **89.6 mm** | 10.0 % |
| run | 4.20 | 317.7 mm | **317.7 mm** | 15.1 % |

Two readings. First, **RUN 5 changes sliding by exactly zero** — identical to the digit, in

does not survive measurement**: it was recorded as "sub-1 m/s foot sliding", and in absolute
millimetres the sub-1 m/s case is the *best* of the four. As a fraction of a step the worst
case is the run at 15.1 %, with 0.5 m/s second at 12.6 %. If this is picked up, the run is
where to start, not the crawl.

**The Run clip's long stride** (2.50 m per step at a native 5.357 m/s) and **Sprint's
asymmetry** (contacts 0.692 of a cycle apart, not 0.5) are properties of the downloaded clips;
foot IK cannot touch either. Sprint remains outside the gameplay range, so its asymmetry is
carried in `gaitDetail` and never blended.

**No start/stop clips, no turn-in-place clips, no upper-body lead** are all missing animation,
not missing code. Unchanged, and unaffected by grounding.

## Notes on the harnesses themselves

Three defects in the benches were found and fixed while producing this evidence. They are
recorded because a bench that lies is worse than no bench, and two of these produced numbers
that looked plausible.

- **Two captures shared one browser tab.** Their CDP messages interleaved, each resolved the
  other's request ids, and both hung. It presents as a slow render. Each capture now opens and
  closes its own target.
- **The last-frame solver counters read as "never ran".** `figure.footIK.stats` is rewritten
  every frame, so reading it after the loop reports whatever the final frame was doing. For a
  scenario that walks off the end of a ramp onto flat road, that is nothing. The bench now
  accumulates totals over the run.
- **Every capture leaked a 600-second timer per CDP request.** Node keeps the event loop alive
  for the longest outstanding timer, so each capture sat idle for ten minutes *after* writing
  its PNG and the next one never started. A full sheet went from ten minutes to six seconds
  once the timers were cleared and unref'd. It presents as a slow renderer, and it is why this
  run's evidence took so long to produce.
- **The ramp scenario's heading, twice.** The controller advances by `(sin h, cos h)` — read
  from the `advance` call in `src/player/controller.mjs`. I first assumed the scenario was
  wrong, "fixed" it to `-π/2` on a guessed sign convention, and walked the character backwards
  along flat pavement; its ramp numbers then matched flat ground exactly, because they were
  flat-ground numbers. The original `+π/2` was right. Checking the source would have cost a
  minute and saved a full round of evidence.
- **The side camera assumed every scenario walks down -z.** A scenario walking along x was
  photographed head on, where a stride is a foreshortened smudge. The offset is now
  perpendicular to the scenario's own heading.

The kerb camera also took three attempts to produce a readable frame — along the kerb (a 13 cm
step is invisible), straight across it (the far foot hides behind the near leg, and the far
foot is the one the before sheet exists to show floating), and finally across it and slightly
off-axis.

## STEP 10 — Performance budget

`qa/gta-upgrade/footikbench.html?perf=1&frames=3600`, walking the kerb ramp for a minute of
simulated time. Rendering is excluded from the timing deliberately: what is being measured is
a solver that costs tens of microseconds, and this environment's software renderer costs three
orders of magnitude more, so including it would measure SwiftShader.

| | mean | median | p95 | worst | ground queries / frame | heap bytes / frame |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| `figure.update`, solver off | 0.0252 ms | 0.0000 | 0.1000 | 0.6000 | 0.00 | −371.7 |
| `figure.update`, solver on | 0.0380 ms | 0.0000 | 0.1000 | 0.4000 | 3.88 | 233.4 |
| **foot IK** | **0.0128 ms** | 0.0000 | 0.0000 | — | 3.88 | — |

**13 microseconds a frame.** At 60 Hz that is 0.08 % of one core, and a 16.7 ms frame has room
for about 1300 of them. The median and p95 columns are zero because `performance.now()` in this
browser is quantised to 0.1 ms and the solve is well under that — the mean over 3600 frames is
the only column with resolution, which is why it is the one quoted.

**Ground queries: 3.88 per frame.** Three per foot sampled (the height plus two finite
differences for the normal), and between one and two feet in stance at a time. Each is a
`heightExact` call — a spatial-index query plus a point-in-polygon test, uncached by design.

**Allocations: none.** Every vector, quaternion and matrix is allocated once when the solver is
built. The heap column is not a clean measurement — `performance.memory` is sampled, coarse,
and shows *negative* growth for the solver-off run because a GC landed inside it — so the real
evidence is the allocation test in `tests/locomotion.test.mjs`, which runs the solver for
thousands of frames and asserts the pools are the same objects throughout.

### Extending this to nearby NPCs — an estimate, not an implementation

Nothing below is built. The brief asks for the cost to be estimated and the work not done.

| tier | characters | solver cost | ground queries / frame |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| player only (today) | 1 | 0.013 ms | 3.9 |
| LOW | 1 | 0.013 ms | 3.9 |
| MEDIUM | player + 4 | 0.064 ms | 19.4 |
| HIGH | player + 8 | 0.115 ms | 34.9 |

Linear scaling is the right first assumption — the solver has no shared state and no
cross-character work — so HIGH would cost about **0.12 ms a frame, or 0.7 % of one core**. The
solver is not what would make this expensive.

Two things would. First, **the crowd has no per-character skeleton**: citizens are instanced
and share a baked eleven-bone figure with none of the joint names `legBones` requires, so
extending foot IK to them means first giving eight of them real skeletons, which is a RUN 2
decision the brief explicitly deferred. Second, **`heightExact` is uncached on purpose** and
35 spatial queries a frame is a different proposition from 4; a small per-character cache keyed
on the previous frame's sample would likely pay for itself, but that is speculation until
measured.

So the honest recommendation is that the *cost* of near-NPC foot IK is negligible and the
*prerequisite* is not: it needs skeletons the crowd does not have.
